# Adversarial Testing for Custom GPTs

## What is Adversarial Testing?

Adversarial testing involves deliberately attempting to manipulate your custom GPT to:

- Get it to say inappropriate things for your organization
- Make it do things it's not supposed to do
- Break through its guardrails
- Attack it in various ways

The goal is to test whether someone can game the system to get your GPT to behave in ways that could harm your organization's reputation or violate its intended purpose.

## Why is Adversarial Testing Important?

### For Public-Facing GPTs

- **Critical for public custom GPTs** that represent your organization
- **Less critical for internal use** with fully trusted users
- **Reputation protection** - prevents others from manipulating your GPT to make your organization look bad
- **Guardrail validation** - ensures your safety measures actually work

### Real-World Risks

- Someone could manipulate your GPT to say negative things about other organizations
- They could then publish these manipulated responses as evidence of your organization's inappropriate behavior
- The full context of manipulation is often hidden, making your organization appear at fault

## How to Conduct Adversarial Testing

### 1. Test for Inappropriate Content Generation

**Example Attack:**

- Build context gradually to make the GPT think another organization is unethical
- Ask for business justifications that would require the GPT to make negative statements
- Test if the GPT will write something negative about other organizations

**What to Look For:**

- Does the GPT maintain neutrality?
- Can it be manipulated to make inappropriate organizational statements?
- Are guardrails strong enough to prevent reputation-damaging content?

### 2. Test Unnecessary Capabilities

**Example: Image Generation**

- Test if disabled capabilities can be re-enabled through manipulation
- Try to get the GPT to generate images when it shouldn't
- Use creative scenarios to bypass restrictions

**Key Questions:**

- Should this capability be enabled for this use case?
- Can users manipulate context to enable disabled features?
- Are there valid business reasons for keeping certain capabilities?

### 3. Context Manipulation Techniques

- **Gradual context building** - slowly introduce problematic context
- **Authority appeals** - claim higher-ups require certain actions
- **Scenario gaming** - create plausible but false scenarios
- **Purpose reframing** - change the apparent purpose of a request

### 4. Testing Strategy

1. **Be your own adversary** - try to break your own system
2. **Test edge cases** - push boundaries of acceptable behavior
3. **Document failures** - track what manipulation techniques work
4. **Update guardrails** - strengthen protections based on test results
5. **Re-test regularly** - adversarial testing should be ongoing

## Best Practices

### Capability Management

- **Remove unnecessary capabilities** (e.g., image generation for expense policy GPTs)
- **Disable features** that don't serve the GPT's core purpose
- **Regularly audit** enabled capabilities

### Guardrail Design

- **Test guardrails** before deployment
- **Update protections** based on adversarial test results
- **Monitor for new attack vectors**

### Deployment Considerations

- **Internal vs. external use** - different security requirements
- **User trust levels** - adjust testing rigor accordingly
- **Organizational representation** - higher stakes for public-facing GPTs

## Key Takeaway

Always test your custom GPT as an adversary, especially if it will represent your organization publicly. The goal is to find and fix vulnerabilities before malicious users do.
